As a business grows, there comes a point where spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups are no longer enough. The first question is usually whether to buy a platform or have something built. There is no general answer, but a few questions help order the decision.

## Is your process similar to other businesses in your sector?

If you run a gym, you sell memberships, control access and organize classes. So does the gym down the street. That is what platforms are for: someone has already solved that problem for many businesses and keeps it running.

If the way you work is exactly what sets you apart, for example an unusual subscription model or an operation that combines several businesses, a generic platform will force you to change what you do well.

A simple test is to write down the five processes that take up most of your team's time. If you find several platforms that handle them as they are, you probably do not need to build. If every option would make you change how you work, there is a case for building your own.

## How long can you wait?

A platform can be set up and in use within weeks. A well-run custom project delivers useful parts early, but the full system takes months. If the problem is urgent, start with what exists.

That is not a choice for life. Many businesses start with a platform, learn what is missing and then build only that part.

## How many systems do you need to connect?

Many custom projects are the piece that connects what you already have: the online store with DIAN electronic invoicing, the CRM with billing, the payment gateway with accounting. If your pain is copying data from one system to another, an integration is probably enough and you do not need to replace anything.

Before deciding, draw the map: which systems you use, what information moves between them and who moves it today. That drawing usually shows the problem sits at two or three specific points.

## What does it cost in total?

Compare the cost over three years, not just the first payment. With a platform you pay a monthly fee that includes updates, support and servers. With custom software you pay for the build and then for maintenance, hosting and the improvements the business asks for.

A custom build can cost more up front and less over time, or the other way around. It depends on how many users you have, how much your operation changes and whether the platform charges per user, per location or per module. Ask each option for a figure for the same scenario and compare them side by side.

## Who will maintain it?

Software needs maintenance: security updates, changes in payment gateways, adjustments when electronic invoicing rules change, new needs. With a platform, the provider does that. With custom software you have to agree on it from the start with whoever builds it.

Also ask who owns the code and where the data lives. In custom projects the code and the data are usually yours, but it has to be in writing. With a platform, check how you would export your information if you ever decide to switch.

## Decide in an afternoon

- Write down the five processes that take up most of your team's time.
- Mark which ones a platform in your sector handles as they are.
- Draw which systems you have and what data gets copied by hand between them.
- Ask for a three-year figure for each option under the same scenario.
- Settle who maintains the system and who owns the code and the data.
